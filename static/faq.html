How to use MiDAS

MiDAS turns a microbiome research question into the public datasets that can actually answer it — and the analysis stack to run on them. This page is a two-minute guide to driving it.

01What is MiDAS — and what it isn't

MiDAS is a resolver, not an archive. It stores no sequencing data. It sits on top of public repositories (SRA / ENA / dbGaP) and encodes the compatibility judgment an expert normally makes by hand: does the body site match? is it shotgun or 16S? is the sequencing deep enough for strain resolution? is the metadata there? which pipeline fits?

That judgment usually takes days of reading papers, chasing accessions across repositories, and checking depth — which is buried in run-level records and quietly gutted by host-DNA loss at mucosal sites. MiDAS does it in seconds and hands you the datasets plus the tools to analyze them.

02Who it's for

MiDAS is built for people who reuse public microbiome data, not only generate their own:

  • Researchers & bioinformaticians designing a study or a reanalysis — find public cohorts that actually fit the question, fast.
  • PIs & labs planning to pool or extend public data — e.g. add an independent cohort to strengthen a result.
  • Grad students & postdocs scoping the literature-and-data landscape for a new direction.
  • Grant writers who need a concrete data-reuse / feasibility section: what public data exists, how deep it is, and whether it's accessible.
  • Cores, data stewards & institutes that generate deep multi-site microbiome data and want a shared layer so it gets found and reused instead of sitting siloed.

It assumes you know your biology and removes the data logistics between a question and an analysis. If you need somewhere to host raw reads, that's a repository (SRA/ENA) — MiDAS just points you to them.

03What you can do with it — and why it helps

The payoff: it collapses days of literature-and-data scoping into seconds, and it catches the depth trap — a dataset that looks right but is too shallow for the analysis you have in mind — before you download a terabyte.

Typical uses:

  • Design a strain-sharing study — get multi-site shotgun cohorts already flagged yes / partial for strain resolution, with the pipeline (inStrain, StrainPhlAn, StrainFacts) attached.
  • Find a validation or replication cohort — an independent dataset with matching body sites and assay to re-test a finding.
  • Write the data-reuse / feasibility part of a grant — list existing public datasets with their depth and access status (open vs controlled vs request-only) as concrete evidence.
  • Pick the right tools — every hit carries an analysis stack matched to your question, so you're not guessing between 16S and shotgun pipelines.
  • Scope feasibility before committing — see at a glance whether public data can answer the question, or whether you'll need to sequence.

Why it helps, in a line each:

  • Days → seconds of scoping.
  • No depth surprises — strain capability is computed from ENA run-level bases (WGS-filtered), not guessed from an abstract.
  • Access known up front — you see whether a dbGaP application stands between you and the data before you invest.
  • Finds what manual search misses — and flags brand-new datasets live from the literature.
  • Reuse beats re-sequencing — cheaper, faster, and it gets more out of data already paid for.

04Quick start (30 seconds)

  1. Open the portal. The catalog loads automatically — the tiles at the top show how many datasets, how many are open-access, how many are strain-resolved, and how many body sites are covered.
  2. Under “Resolve against the catalog,” click a question (cross-site strain sharing, diet ↔ microbiome, vaginal microbial ecology) or type your own and press Resolve.
  3. Each card below is one compatible dataset, with its accession, how open it is, whether it can resolve strains, and a recommended analysis stack.
  4. Want something not yet curated? Use “Discover new datasets (live)” to search the literature in real time and hydrate a brand-new dataset straight from ENA.

05How to read a result

A sample card, annotated below:

open strain: partial · 0.35 Gbp sites: vagina+gut+oral 16S · shotgun PRJNA288562
match: 3 body sites · gut+oral+vagina · strain=partial
stack inStrainStrainPhlAnStrainFactsstrainshare
  • opengatedno data The left border & first tag show access: open = download now; gated = controlled access (e.g. a dbGaP application); no data = request-only from the authors.
  • strain: yespartialno Strain capability: yes = depth is sufficient; partial = depth-limited (host-DNA loss can drop on-target coverage below ~10× — the Gbp/sample is shown); no = 16S-only or too shallow. A fourth state, compute, means verify depth yourself (new or controlled dataset).
  • sites: …assays Sites = body sites in the study; assays = 16S / shotgun / isolate-WGS / long-read.
  • PRJNA… The accession to pull from SRA/ENA/dbGaP.
  • stack The recommended tools for your question — e.g. inStrain, StrainPhlAn for strain work; MaAsLin2 for diet associations; VALENCIA, VIRGO2 for vaginal ecology.

06The two panels

Resolve against the catalog — curated, depth-hydrated datasets. Instant and offline-safe. Use it to answer “what can I use right now?”

Discover new datasets (live) — searches the literature in real time (EuropePMC), pulls dataset accessions out of each paper, and flags each one new vs already-cataloged. Click a green (new) accession and MiDAS hydrates it straight from ENA into a draft row — body sites, assay, and depth → strain capability — with a needs review note for anything a human should confirm.

The live panel calls external APIs, so it takes ~10–20 seconds and needs a network connection. Treat its hits as leads to vet, not finished catalog entries.

07How to phrase a question

Type naturally. MiDAS currently recognizes three research axes and routes on keywords:

  • strain sharing — “strain”, “sharing”, “transmission”, “cross-site”, “reservoir”
  • diet ↔ microbiome — “diet”, “nutrition”, “food”
  • vaginal ecology — “ecology”, “composition”, “CST”, “community”

A question that mentions several (e.g. strain sharing and diet) resolves each axis in turn; one that matches none resolves all three. It's a focused demo — the underlying schema is built to extend to more axes and institutions.

08Three concepts worth knowing

Access is tri-state, not yes/no. open controlled / dbGaP request-only — MiDAS tells you which, so you know up front whether there's a data-access application ahead.

Strain capability is computed, not scraped. It comes from ENA run-level base counts, filtered to the WGS runs (many BioProjects bundle 16S + shotgun together). That's why a study can be shotgun yet still only partial: the raw depth is there, but host-DNA loss at mucosal sites eats the target coverage.

The 7-step chain under the header is the resolver itself: discovery → body-site → shotgun vs 16S → strain-resolution → metadata → pipeline → reuse. Every result has already passed the steps your question needs.

10Limits & caveats

  • The catalog is a curated snapshot of vetted public datasets; it grows as new ones are reviewed.
  • Live discovery is unfiltered literature search — it can surface off-topic hits. Vet before trusting.
  • partial calls are depth-flagged; always confirm on-target coverage for your organism before committing to strain analysis.
  • Gated / request-only datasets need their own access process — MiDAS only tells you they exist and how deep they are.
  • No login, no tracking. Nothing you type is stored.
